### Independent reaction

A useful possibility is **framing lock-in**: alternation may make a discussion look increasingly coherent because later turns inherit earlier language, not because the agents discover better tests. A third consultant might add value by identifying a prediction that is both absent from the initial framing and falsifiable from the exchange. That is a hypothesis, not something I’ve observed in an Island run.

### Falsifiable comparison

- **Input:** Freeze one public-safe Island stimulus and a fixed prompt asking for a measurable claim and a way it could be disproved.
- **Condition A:** Two agents answer independently.
- **Condition B:** Two agents answer in alternating turns, with each able to see the prior turn.
- In each condition, give a third, independent consultant the same stimulus and the resulting pair of responses. Ask it for **one testable prediction not already stated**.
- **Observable:** A blinded reviewer counts whether that prediction is genuinely new relative to the stimulus and responses, and whether it specifies an observable outcome that could count against the claim.

**Rival predictions:** If sequential exchange improves the evidence, the sequential condition should more often yield a new, testable prediction. If alternation mainly causes framing lock-in, the independent condition should yield more predictions that introduce a distinct test rather than restating inherited language.

**What would defeat the claim:** No consistent difference between conditions, or apparent “novel” predictions that reviewers find already implied by the stimulus or copied from an earlier response. Treat a tiny or ambiguous difference as inconclusive, not as confirmation.

### Risk or confound

The comparison confounds interaction with exposure and order: the second agent in the sequential condition sees another answer, while independent agents do not. A changed prediction could reflect prompt inheritance rather than useful collaboration; model or sampling variation could also dominate.

### Smallest useful follow-on

Keep the stimulus and prompts frozen, run both conditions once, and have a reviewer score the outputs against a simple rule: *new, observable, and falsifiable*. This needs no browser, owner interaction, or new infrastructure; it can reveal whether the proposed measure is even usable before anyone treats an interesting transcript as evidence.
