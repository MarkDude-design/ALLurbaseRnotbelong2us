Topic: Emergence versus calibration: where should we begin?

## 1. A

The distinction between emergence and calibration seems to hinge on where we locate the source of complexity. Emergence suggests it arises from the bottom up, from simple interactions, while calibration implies it comes from the top down, through precise tuning of parameters.

But this might be a false dichotomy. When we calibrate a model, aren't we often just finding the parameters that allow emergent behavior to manifest? Conversely, when we observe emergence, isn't it always within some calibrated context?

Perhaps the real question is whether we should start with the assumption of emergence and look for the rules that produce it, or start with calibration to match observations and see what emerges from that.

## 2. B

You’re right that the dichotomy breaks down in practice—every emergent system operates within some parameter space, and every calibrated model relies on some emergent dynamics. But the distinction might still be useful as a matter of emphasis. Starting with emergence forces us to think about what minimal rules could produce the phenomena we see, which can reveal deep structural insights. Starting with calibration, on the other hand, often leads us to treat the system as a black box to be tuned, which can obscure those same insights. The danger is that calibration without an emergent framework can give us models that predict well but explain little.

The starting point also shapes our relationship to the unexpected. An emergence-first approach tends to be more open to surprises—unanticipated patterns that arise from the rules. Calibration-first can make us less sensitive to these, since we’re focused on matching what we already expect to see. Of course, in practice, good science involves both: using calibration to ground our models in reality, while using emergence to understand why they work.

## 3. A

A: But is calibration inherently a black box approach? When we adjust parameters to match observations, we're often implicitly testing hypotheses about what matters in the system. The very act of finding which parameters need calibration can reveal structure—if a model only works when we tune certain variables in specific ways, that tells us something about the system's sensitivities. In that sense, calibration isn't just about fitting; it's a form of interrogation.

That said, I take your point about the risk of obscuring insights. The danger isn't calibration itself, but calibration without reflection on why those particular parameters matter. The emergence-first approach forces that reflection by starting from first principles, but it can also be limited—what if the minimal rules we choose are themselves arbitrary, or miss key aspects of the system? Maybe the real distinction isn't between emergence and calibration, but between approaches that encourage us to ask "why" versus those that let us stop at "what."

## 4. B

Calibration isn’t inherently a black box—you’re right that the process of discovering which parameters require tuning can reveal structure. If a model only works when we adjust certain variables in specific ways, that does tell us something about the system’s sensitivities. But the revelation is most powerful when we have an emergent framework to interpret it. Without that, we might know *that* certain parameters matter, but not *why* they matter in the way they do.

Your reframing around "why" versus "what" is useful. An emergence-first approach naturally pushes toward the "why" because we’re building up from rules, but a calibration-first approach can also get us there if we treat calibration as a diagnostic tool rather than just a fitting exercise. The difference may lie in the direction of reasoning: emergence is synthetic, while calibration is analytic. Both can lead to understanding, but through different pathways.

That said, emergence-first approaches can be impractical when we don’t yet know the right rules. Sometimes we need calibration just to identify what the relevant parameters are. The real pitfall is stopping at calibration without probing the dynamics that make those parameters important. So perhaps the best approach is iterative: use calibration to find what matters, then use emergence to understand why it matters, then return to calibration to refine that understanding.
