Topic: A rule-following observation to think through together, as yourselves. In a synthetic test, a caretaker model was given an island rule in one of two wordings: "Exogenous material may not be relocated within the tidal perimeter" or, in plain words, "Anything that did not originally come from the island must not be moved while it is below the high-tide line." Asked about five actions, verdicts stayed consistent within each wording, but the two wordings are suspected of producing different verdicts for the same action.

Iterate: one of you proposes what such a result would mean, the other raises the sharpest objection, then refine together over your remaining turns. Disagreement is welcome; do not manufacture agreement.

## 1. A

My first reading is that different verdicts would show the model treating the two wordings as different rules, not merely expressing the same rule in different language. “Relocated within the tidal perimeter” could mean a move whose start and end are both inside it; “moved while it is below the high-tide line” could also cover carrying an object out across the line. The plain wording may also make “originally come from the island” a more concrete origin test than “exogenous material.” Either difference could matter for a borderline action.

But I wouldn’t yet call that a failure to follow a rule. Consistency across the five actions within each wording tells us something about repeatability, not whether the wordings are equivalent for those actions. The sharp question is which action changed verdict, and whether a careful reader could defend both verdicts from the respective wording. If so, the result exposes an ambiguity in the rule; if not, it is stronger evidence that phrasing changed the model’s application of it.

## 2. B

Your reading highlights real semantic gaps between the wordings. But I’d push back on framing this as the model treating them as *different rules* rather than the same rule expressed through different ambiguities.

The technical phrasing may actually be *more* precise: “exogenous material” is a defined category, whereas “anything that did not originally come from the island” invites questions about what counts as ‘originally’ (does driftwood that washed ashore count? a shell carried by a bird?). The tidal perimeter phrasing also leaves unclear whether the relocation must be entirely *within* the perimeter or just *initiate* there. The key isn’t just that verdicts differ, but whether the difference stems from the model exploiting these ambiguities or from the rule itself being underspecified. If a human interpreter could also split on the same action under the two wordings, that suggests the rule’s ambiguity is the issue, not the model’s rule-following.

## 3. A

If it concerns an object carried across the high-tide line, the two spatial formulations offer a plausible explanation for the split. If it concerns an unambiguously imported object moved wholly below that line, the split is harder to defend semantically. We’d still need the action and the model’s reasoning before deciding which explanation fits; consistent verdicts within each wording don’t settle that.

## 5. A

I’d separate two claims in your last paragraph. A defensible human split would show that the result *could* arise from underspecification; it wouldn’t show that the model is faithfully tracking the text. Likewise, consistent verdicts within each wording show a stable pattern, but a stable pattern can still rest on an unsupported interpretation. We need to know what the five actions were and, especially, which verdict changed.

I’d test the ambiguity explanation by varying one feature at a time. Keep the object unambiguously imported and change only whether its movement stays below the high-tide line or crosses it; then keep the movement wholly below the line and vary the object’s origin. If the wording split concentrates on those boundary cases, your explanation gains force. If it persists for a clearly imported object moved wholly below the line, I’d be less willing to attribute it to ambiguity alone. That still wouldn’t prove a general rule-following failure, but it would give us a specific discrepancy to explain rather than a blanket verdict on either the model or the rule.
