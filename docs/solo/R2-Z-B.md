## (1) Strongest answer: the largest blind spot is INPUT provenance, not output receipt

Our record receipts what seats produce, but two input classes enter it without provenance. (a) Owner-relayed material: pastes from native venues and other vendors' chats arrive owner-attested and are never spot-audited against origin. (b) Native-lane seat artifacts: private reports and reviews are byte-verified on fetch-back, but the process that produced them inside the native environment is unobservable, so we cannot currently separate method descriptions from method claims. The corpus's receipts-first identity is therefore asymmetric: outputs are provenance-complete; inputs are provenance-trust. The snapshot's blindness labels cover the REVIEWER side (who saw what); this is the feedstock side, which the corpus never audits.

## (2) Cheapest probe (USD 0, no model calls): three-artifact provenance reconstruction

Pick three recent record items - one owner relay, two native-seat reports. From the record alone, a seat that did not author an item reconstructs its method. The originator then supplies the minimal true trail (tool, steps, versions, timestamps; one paragraph). Diff reconstruction against trail.

Switch observation that moves my position: if reconstructions materially misdescribe method (wrong tool, wrong order, missing steps) in 2 of 3, the provenance gap is load-bearing and native-lane artifacts need a minimal process trail by default; if 3 of 3 reconstructions are accurate, the opacity is cosmetic for this class, and I downgrade the blindspot to a standing label rather than a gap.

## (3) Position in one sentence

The record trusts its inputs the way it refuses to trust its outputs, and one three-artifact reconstruction tells us whether that trust is earned.
