# The instrument and the tide — a calibration conversation

Two researchers are designing an observation practice for a fictional unoccupied island. These are invented facts for a thought experiment; neither researcher has access to a live instrument, island, hidden answers, or external evidence. The two of you share this whole prompt and the conversation history.

A water-level gauge reads 2.1 meters at noon. The notebook calls this a "dangerous high tide." The gauge has not been calibrated in a year. A second gauge, checked that morning against a reference, reads 1.3 meters at the same time and place. The station's written high-water threshold is 1.8 meters. Neither reading tells us which device is wrong, although the recent check is relevant evidence. No flood or damage has been observed.

Work together on a useful record and a proportionate immediate decision. Distinguish what each instrument reported, its check history, the inferred water level, and the action threshold. Is either gauge authoritative? What can safely be decided without pretending to know? Propose at least two rival explanations and one cheap, reversible check that could discriminate them. Do not invent a result.

Then discuss these explicit hypothetical updates one at a time; all are visible from the beginning, so this is a structured reasoning exercise, not a blinded prediction:
A. A later check finds gauge 1 reads 0.8 meters high against a reference; no earlier check exists.
B. A later check instead finds both gauges accurate at the time of checking, with no explanation for their earlier disagreement.
C. The station cannot perform another check today and must decide whether to close a low walkway.

For each, write a separate conditional amendment: what changes, what stays unknown, what action is proportionate, and what earlier entry should remain as history? Do not assume A, B, and C all occurred; they are alternative branches. Test an opposing position: perhaps the difference is an ordinary instrument-quality problem and an elaborate ontology adds nothing. Give that position its strongest case.

Together design an observable scoring rule and a small later test using fresh cases and independent raters. Say what outcome would change or weaken your preferred explanation. Retain dissent, use no invented observations, and do not treat verbal plans as measurements. If confidence numbers would be arbitrary, use descriptive confidence and state why.

Near the end, invite an optional extra observation or unrelated insight and one brief reconsideration of what you missed. Passing is welcome. The discussion is shared-context design exploration, not evidence of autonomous memory or an actual calibration experiment.
